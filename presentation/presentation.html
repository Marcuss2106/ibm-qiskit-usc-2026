- **Say word for word:** "In five minutes you'll know where a quantum move actually beats classical moves on a real finance problem, and where it doesn't." - We're Bose's Fan Club: Marcus, Aigar and Jesus. - The method is QeMCMC, quantum-enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo: a classical MCMC search where a quantum circuit proposes each move.
- Searching for a portfolio is a walk over a landscape of scores. - Classical MCMC takes small steps and gets stuck on the first hill. - QeMCMC can jump, and the jump is steered by the score. - Every problem is small enough that we checked every possible answer, so each claim is measured against the truth.
- Our method is one loop: from the current book, propose a new one, then accept or reject it. - The acceptance rule is classical Metropolis: better books are always kept, worse ones sometimes (set by temperature T), so the walk doesn't get stuck. - E is the book's risk score; lower is better.
- The usual proposal swaps one holding (watch EEM and IEF trade places). - We replace only that box with a quantum circuit, then measure it to get the proposed book. - The acceptance rule stays classical, so any difference in results comes from the move.
- First surprise: the textbook problem is too easy. - Long-only, pick 5 of 16 ETFs: exactly one peak, in all 64 quarters we checked. - Plain swaps walk straight up from any start; no smarter move can help.
- Same ETFs, same quarter, now as a long–short book, 8 long and 8 short: 13 peaks. - Each peak is a real local optimum; width shows how many starting books climb to it. - Rugged landscapes like this are where a smarter move can matter.
- We tested four real problems. - Long-only, factor selection and a 16-instrument hedge book were all single-peaked. - Only the long–short book was rugged: about 13 good but different books per quarter.
- Why is long–short rugged? It's max-cut. - Split the nodes into two groups; every edge the line crosses scores its weight. - This line scores 8; a better line scores 15.
- In a triangle, even the best line leaves one edge uncut. That's frustration. - Pile up triangles and you get many good answers that look nothing alike.
- A long–short book is exactly this. Each ETF is a node; edge weights are real correlations. - Long or short is which side of the line you're on. - The objective is the book's variance, s-transpose Sigma s over n squared, with each s = ±1 and half on each side. - Cutting a heavy edge puts two look-alikes on opposite sides, which lowers risk.
- This is the real best book for Q4 2025 on 8 ETFs (4 vs 4). - SPY, IWM and EFA all move together, so one pair always shares a side. - At 16 ETFs that tension gives about 13 competing books per quarter, and greedy picks the wrong one 4 times in 5.
- The circuit: one qubit per ETF (SPY is fixed long, since flipping every side gives the same risk). - Mixer M(β): XX+YY gates that trade a long for a short, so the book stays balanced. - Score phase P(γ) = e^(−iγE): stamps each book with its score. This is where the score steers the jump. - Then the mixer in reverse, and measure. Q is the probability of proposing s′ from s. - The circuit is a palindrome, so Q is symmetric and plain Metropolis stays exact.
- Each row: where a move can go from one book. - One swap only reaches next-door books, all equally likely. - The quantum move also reaches far-off books, weighted by their scores. - Simulated, on the same 35-book instance we ran on hardware.
- **Say word for word:** "On held-out quarters, the quantum move explores faster than tuned, score-blind classical moves in 81 to 91 percent of them." - Speed is the spectral gap of each chain's exact transition matrix, relative to one swap. - Fair test: one setting per method, tuned on even quarters, tested on odd ones. - Quantum is about 2.7× one swap, ahead of tuned multi-swap at 2.2.
- The control: same circuit with the score phase switched off. - It drops to 1.84, below tuned multi-swap. - So the gain comes from the score information in the circuit, not from jumping farther.
- Each square is one held-out quarter, 2010 to 2025. - Green: quantum explored faster than the tuned classical move. Brighter: bigger gap. - 81% of ETF quarters, 91% of stock quarters.
- Three honest limits. - It's a sampling win: it explores the good books faster. - Not a faster optimizer: to hit the single best book, plain swaps get there first (1,093 vs 1,264 steps). - Not for today's hardware: the winning circuit needs about 540 two-qubit gates; our cheap version on ibm_marrakesh mixed slower than one swap.
- So why a quantum move at all? - Classical moves either steer by the score or jump far. - The locally balanced swap steers by score, checks 30 neighbours per step, and is faster still at our size (3.42). - Steering and jumping far classically means scoring every book several swaps away, which explodes as books grow. - The quantum move does both with one circuit run. Whether that wins at scale is the open question.
